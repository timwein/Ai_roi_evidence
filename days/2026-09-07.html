John Wiley & Sons, Inc. Media / Scholarly publishing · US S3 candidate (unproven)⚠ Counter-evidence

In the Q1 FY2027 earnings release filed as an SEC Form 8-K exhibit (3 Sep 2026) Wiley states "AI licensing revenue totaled $14 million for the quarter", against prior-year AI licensing revenue of $29 million - down 52% year over year. The release breaks the comparison out by segment: Research Solutions AI licensing was "$16 million vs. $4 million this quarter", and the prior-year Learning comparisons were $8 million in Academic and $5 million in Professional. On the call the same day CEO Matthew Kissner said "Of the $14 million, $10.5 million is from model training and $3.5 million is recurring", that Wiley has "contracted a further $14 million of AI licensing revenue that will be realized across Q2 and Q3", and that the company remains "well on track for over $50 million in fiscal 2027". At Q4 FY2026 (16 Jun 2026) Kissner had disclosed the annual series: "We grew AI revenue from 40 million to 49 million with a rapidly expanding recurring base", with the recurring piece "rapidly scaling from roughly 1 million last year to 8 million in fiscal 26".

Mechanism
Two distinct revenue lines sit inside the single "AI licensing" label, and they behave differently. The first is one-time model-training licensing: Wiley sells rights to its journal corpus and backfile to AI developers training foundation models, which is episodic, deal-shaped and non-recurring. The second is a subscription business Wiley built - knowledge feeds sold to 23 corporate customers across five verticals, and Nexus, a licensing service Wiley operates on behalf of 71 third-party publisher partners. Only the second is a product; the first is rent on an asset Wiley already owned.
Why this shape
S3-CANDIDATE, and the candidate flag is doing real work. Test (a) counterfactual purchase: the buyers are AI model developers and corporate AI teams, who previously bought nothing from Wiley - passes. Test (b) prior alternative: the prior alternative was uncompensated scraping or going without, not a competitor's product - passes. Test (c) capability gate: the demand requires frontier LLM training, which did not exist as a buying category ~24 months before the earliest of these deals - passes on the demand side. Test (d) newly economic: yes for the recurring feeds, which had no viable buyer before. What fails is the question that sits behind all four: this is not ROI on Wiley's own AI spend. Wiley is monetising a pre-existing corpus because AI labs became buyers - structurally the same as a genset maker selling into the data-centre build-out, which the standing rule excludes as AI-as-customer rather than AI-as-capability. The recurring half is the part with a genuine claim to being a new SKU with no predecessor in Wiley's catalog. It is logged rather than dropped because it is the cleanest dated, SEC-filed, falsifiable test available of whether AI-adjacent revenue at a non-tech incumbent is durable. Marked counter_evidence because the headline answer this quarter is no: the line halved. The nuance that keeps it from being purely negative is that the decline is entirely in the lumpy one-time component, while the recurring base compounds - roughly $1m (FY25) to $8m (FY26) to $3.5m in a single quarter, about a $14m annualised run rate.

Falsifier
Wiley needs roughly $22m of AI revenue in Q4 FY2027 and beyond what is contracted to reach the "over $50 million" target, since only $14m realised plus $14m contracted is visible. If FY2027 AI revenue lands below $49m - i.e. below FY2026 - the new-TAM reading is dead and this was a two-year pull-forward of corpus rights. Second falsifier, independent of the first: if the recurring component fails to reach roughly $16m for FY2027 (the low end of Kissner's own "2x to 3x" the $8m FY2026 base), then the durable product half is not compounding and the whole line is one-time licensing with a subscription veneer.
