Assessment

Eight findings, no S3 for the sixth consecutive run. The day's real result is that two falsifiers this log wrote months ago came due this week and both fired: Infosys nearly doubled its AI revenue share while cutting its growth guidance, and Ryan Specialty replaced last quarter's AI throughput metric with a different one rather than converting it.

This run covers a seven-day gap - the last logged day was 2026-10-23 - so the sweep was widened to the full week, which was the back half of Q3 earnings season. Every figure below was read this run in a document retrieved this run; two were pulled from Alphabet's own transcript PDF rather than a secondary rendering.

Cross-run pattern, extended by a third mechanism this week: the conversion step is where AI revenue claims die, and firms rotate their metrics rather than retire them. Ryan Specialty has now given three different AI throughput statistics in three quarters and never the same one twice. BMS restated a target under questioning and declined to quantify delivery. Customers Bancorp, last week, migrated from revenue to cost inside one quarter. A metric that is never repeated cannot be falsified, which is the function it serves.
Second pattern, and the sharpest datapoint of the day: Infosys proves the substitution case arithmetically. AI revenue share went 5.5% to 8.2% to 10.5% across three quarters while total growth guidance was cut to 1% to 2%. Those two facts can only coexist if AI-labelled work is displacing non-AI-labelled work in the same accounts. The CEO calls it "new revenue" in the same call in which he cuts the number that would evidence it, and withholds the year-on-year.
Third: the firms with the best instrumentation report the softest units. Alphabet quantifies its ads AI as "incremental advertiser value" - value delivered to its customers, not revenue in its own accounts - and does not define it. If the largest AI capex programme in the world reports in value units, expecting hard revenue attribution from a steelmaker or a grocer is not a reasonable baseline.

Federal Reserve Bank of New York - 'AI Adoption and Employment Expectations: Evidence from a Survey of Small Business Owners' (2025 Small Business Credit Survey, 5,248 US employer firms in the AI module, fielded Sep-Nov 2025) Cross-sector (US small business) · US S2 — Share⚠ Counter-evidence

Liberty Street Economics post by Will Aarons and Asani Sarkar, published 8 Oct 2026 - three weeks old and unlogged until now - reporting the only large realised revenue number retrieved this run: "Only 31 percent of survey respondents reported increased sales from their firm's use of AI." On the cost side, "77 percent of respondents reported no change in their firm's current labor costs from the use of AI." Everything else in the post is expectations: AI users show a net expectation of higher revenue exceeding non-users by 48 against 21 percentage points raw, falling to "a 14 percentage point advantage over non-users in net expected revenue growth" after controlling for firm and owner characteristics and past-twelve-month revenue changes. The authors state the outcome is unmeasured: "Whether AI-adopting firms actually experience the stronger revenue and employment outcomes they anticipate is a subject for future research."

Mechanism
Self-reported AI use across a nationally weighted sample of US employer firms, cross-tabulated against self-reported sales change and against twelve-month-forward revenue and employment expectations, with regression controls for firm and owner characteristics and prior-period outcomes.
Why this shape
S2 because the realised finding concerns increased sales of existing products by existing firms into existing markets, with no counterfactual establishing that any of it is new demand. Counter-evidence because the headline cuts against the adoption narrative in two directions at once: among firms that actually use AI, fewer than one in three saw sales rise, and more than three in four saw no change in labour costs - so on the Fed's own numbers the modal small-business AI deployment moves neither the top line nor the cost line. The 14-point expectations gap is the figure that will be quoted; it measures optimism, and the authors say plainly that whether it converts is unknown.
